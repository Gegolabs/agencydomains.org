The Real-Time Enterprise · the four measures

Four numbers you can start measuring on Monday

One for each face of the real-time enterprise. No tool required: a calendar, sent mail and a one-question survey are enough.

Most organizations measure their communication the way social networks do: messages, response times, engagement. Those numbers count activity on the pipe. They say nothing about whether a question got answered, whether anyone had three quiet hours, or whether the day ended. The Real-Time Enterprise proposes four outcome measures instead, one for each way people still serve as the organization’s infrastructure.

FaceThe measureHow to take it this week, by hand
The Quantum Leap from weeks to seconds Log every business question your leadership team asks of its data: when it was asked, when an answer good enough to decide arrived, and how many people it passed through. Report the median and the share still open.
Postchat hours of uninterrupted work protected per person per day Ask a sample of people at the end of each day: “Today, how many hours did you work without being interrupted?” Report the median by team.
The General Staff the fraction of management time that was transport Managers mark a week of their own calendar as transport (status meetings, relay reports, chasing acknowledgments) or judgment. Report transport hours over management hours.
The Watch the hour at which the day truly ends Note the time of each person’s last work message or call of the day, or ask them. Report the median and how often it falls after the official close.

From the book’s executive agenda: “Your organization’s current numbers will be uncomfortable; that is the point. What gets measured becomes enforceable.”

Two rules keep the numbers honest. Report them by team, never by person: when the protected hours are few, the book places the fault in the infrastructure, not in the worker. And keep the activity metrics out of it. A system that is busy on the channel can still score badly on all four.
